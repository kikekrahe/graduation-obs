# Godfrey, Mark - The Artist as Historian

[4]

> Jeremy Deller's The Battle of Orgreave (2001) was a particularly interesting example: Deller organised the re-staging of a clash between police and miners from the miners' strike in 1984. The work resurrected the repressed memory of troubled period of recent British history and by involving protagonists from the clash, Deller also triggered very personal confrontations with the past.

The artist has the capacity to act not only as an archivist and researcher of history, but can evoke this through his trade. Through evocative details and the right target audience, forgotten instances of the past (of which there are many), objects and performances can act as a re-staging of this specific time period. This is especially the case if the referred to occurrence is one of confrontation.
Think for example about Saving Private Ryan's opening sequence.

[5]

> [...] perhaps it is the approaching digitalisation of all photographic mediums that sensitises artists to the way in which such mediums used to serve as records of the past – and this sensitivity provokes artists to make work about the past.

The approach of new technologies and upgrading of techniques from their past usage instills a certain sensitivity and interest in diving into what they were before these changes. Their very change becomes a reason to look back on them.

> On the one hand, globalised capitalist culture is increasingly amnesiac, increasingly focused on ever new markets, products, and experiences. On the other hand, this very culture produces ever more spectacular and romantic representations of the past – particularly in film.

Capitalist culture seems to have a tendency to push forward rather than to look backward. However, this looking backward is still done but with rose glasses, a lens that pacifies and entertains instead of represent. 

> 'The numerous historical films that merely illustrate the past... are attempts at deception according to their own terms. Since one always runs the danger, when picturing current events, of turning easily excitable masses against powerful institutions that are in fact often not appealing, one prefers to direct the camera toward a Middle Ages that the audience will find harmlessly edifying. The further back the story is situated historically, the more audacious filmmakers become. They will risk depicting a successful revolution in historical costumes in order to induce people to forget modern revolutions, and they are happy to satisfy the theoretical sense of justice by filming struggles for freedom that are long past.'

The further into the past a piece of content is, the more it is deemed editable and less verifiable it becomes. The past is used as a tool of instruction and criticism since the present is too politically fragile.

[6]

> Benjamin describes the vanishing point of history as always being the present moment. This formulation of history [...] is an interesting way of reversing the more received notion of history as something which seems to be vanishing somewhere behind us, vanishing into a non-existent time, a time that no longer exists. [...] It forces us to confront history as a construction. It implies that when we reconsider past events, we're not so much going to another time and retrieving material or events. We are restaging those events here and mow in order to think about what's happening here and now, to think about the present.

> To articulate the past historically does not mean to recognise it 'the way it really was'. It means to seize hold of a memory as it flashes up at a moment of danger.

The notion of history being a construction which is summoned in the present moment within your present context as a reflection of the now. 

[8]

> Buckingham acknowledges that fluent historical narratives tend to conceal the power of the narrator and tend not to make explicit what is selected or not selected for inclusion. When he addresses subjects of urgency to the present moment, he recognises that it is not just necessary to present new revisionist narratives, but to reconsider the very role of narrative in historical representation.

The questioning of narratives in historical representation is an interesting point, adding not only to the way in which the information is told but also which information is told and by whom and under what circumstances. It introduces another dimension for historiography apart from the existing historical data, calling its possible reconfiguration into question.

[21]

> First, the artist as historian is just as concerned with a particular historical subject [...] as they are with addressing the history of their mediums and forms.

Keeping in mind that historical subject is only one part of the conversation and that the form of this narrative is equally important.
