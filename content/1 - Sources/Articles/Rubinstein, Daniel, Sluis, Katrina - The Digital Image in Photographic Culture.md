# Rubinstein, Daniel, Sluis, Katrina - The Digital Image in Photographic Culture

> [S]ince Plato charted the course of Western civilization with the simile of the cave, the human being is someone who mistakes truth for the image on the wall. However, in the age of late capitalism, it is the image that places modern man's existence as a living being in question.

We mistake the representation for the truth, the image for the reality.

> For earlier writers and artists of the Baroque, the Renaissance and even for the Greeks, representation was an allegorical image, an illustration or a metaphor, but after Descartes it acquired the status of scientific method capable of producing objective, verifiable and repeatable results.

The Enlightenment are a marking point for representation to be ubiquitous as a source of reality and truth; the world becomes an image.

> Representation therefore performs a dual function: on the one hand it establishes the human subject as a rational being capable of objectifying the world, yet on the other hand it limits the ability to know the world only to those aspects of it that can be rationally represented.

Similarly, AI image generation reduces its generations only to that which can be probabilistically represented, that which is likely and rational. 

> **When a photograph, film or song is only one of many possible 'option[s] inscribed by the algorithms' it becomes harder to talk about photography as a singular cultural form. Instead, the photograph is now a type of 'algorithmic image'; a term we use in order to indicate that the image has to be considered as a kind of program, a process expressed *as, with in* or *through* software.**

> **However, what comes instead of causality is not chaos or randomness, for the dissolution of indexicality does not render the image as meaningless noise; rather, the image is both formed and deformed at the same time through the agency of computation**

> [Because of the pornographic effect caused by proliferation and abundance] it becomes misleading to talk about the photographic 'frame' or the singular image as the image is everywhere all at once, accessible from any point in the network, establishing a regime of intoxication and plentitude through its rapid multiplication

Here Rubinstein and Sluis suggest that, as is backed by Kipnis, the image's rapid proliferation, distribution and multiplication in the digital space is reflective of a larger societal symptom of pleasure in abundance, such as in pornography. The image as an individual, as a singular, virtually exists everywhere and it cannot be pinpointed due to this. 
With this networking and fragmentation of the image, the notion of the image as an archive of a moment in time seems to break down. Instead, there is a 'shift away from content to the rhythm, circulation, and proliferation of the utterance, which, as Jodi Dean notes, 'exerts a force, a compulsion; repetition has effects independent of the meaning of what is repeated' (2010: 26)' (31). 

> The networked image is undecidable, as opposed to 'immaterial' or 'indeterminate' because the latter implies nothingness and absence, while the former names the incomplete and the unresolved. The networked image is undecidable because the meaning of the image is not fixed to any specific event but to the progressive accumulation of a 'data shadow' that determines its visibility and currency in a range of situations.

This data shadow and undecidability is a perfect description of the networked image in the context of artificial intelligence generation. Although it is used to describe digital images, it relies more on the dynamic and generational qualities thereof, something which comes to light especially with the introduction of AI. 

> For instance, the practice of '[[folksonomy]]' opens the image to an ongoing negotiation of meaning creation through tagging, commenting and syndication. [...] Due to the participatory character of folksonomy, tagging transfers the burden of responsibility for the meaning of the image from the figure of the photographer to each and every subject (both human and non human) that comes into contact with the image.

Artificial Intelligence relies on folksonomy to make meaning and give context to machines to interpret the link between image and meaning, often done by Mechanical Turks in precarious working conditions. The networked image, as the AI image, are products of classification and identifications done by both human and non human actors that interpret the image which was framed by a photographer at some point. 

> **While the algorithmic process undermines the iconicity of the image by breaking the causal relation between the representation and the thing represented, images are increasingly presented to the eye with overwhelming richness and lusciousness, on screens designed for hyperrealism (retina displays, 4K screens, 3D cinema).**

> **The paradox of the algorithmic turn, therefore, is that while the image becomes undecidable and its meaning ever more elusive, it also becomes more visually seductive and tactile.**

Kittler argues that the digital image is mathematical, immaterial and devoid of semiotic representations, however still rich in a sensual and affective sense. 
The digital image's reality, rather than being captured, is calculated and constructed, rather than being indexically related to this reality.
This is defined as the after-image, an image which lacks representational qualities but still manages to have a powerful affective force to us.
